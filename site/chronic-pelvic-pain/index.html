Chronic pelvic pain from veins: every option

Reviewed by Abhishek Goswami, MD, interventional radiology · November 12, 2026

Long-term pelvic pain can have many causes. Sometimes the cause is the veins in the pelvis and it can go unrecognized. If you have tried many things and still have no answer, this is an option to look into.

What is pelvic venous disease (pelvic congestion syndrome)?

Pelvic pain that lasts more than 6 months is called chronic pelvic pain. Sometimes it comes from veins in the pelvis that are wide or squeezed. Doctors call this pelvic venous disease. You may also hear the name pelvic congestion syndrome, or May-Thurner syndrome when a large vein is squeezed. It is most common in women before menopause who have been pregnant.1– to 3

Common symptoms3

  • A dull ache
  • Worse when you stand
  • Worse late in the day
  • Worse after sex
  • Worse before your period
When to see a doctor

See a doctor about pelvic pain that keeps coming back. Pelvic pain often has more than one cause, such as endometriosis, and a doctor can check for each one.4, 5

About pelvic vein embolization and stenting

Two vein problems can cause this pain, and they often happen together. Veins near the ovaries and uterus can widen and let blood flow the wrong way. A large vein in the pelvis can also be squeezed by an artery, which is often called May-Thurner syndrome. Each problem has its own treatment. Both are done through a tiny skin nick, without surgery, and nothing is cut out of your body. Some women need one treatment, and some need both.11, 18– to 21

Embolization: closing the wide veins

A doctor guides a thin tube through a tiny skin nick to the wide veins near the ovaries and uterus. Tiny coils, plugs, a medical glue, or a medicine then close those veins.10

What is embolization?

Embolization means blocking blood flow on purpose, from inside a blood vessel. A doctor guides a thin tube to the small blood vessels that feed the problem area. Then the doctor releases a material that blocks them.6

For pelvic veins, the doctor uses tiny metal coils, small wire-mesh plugs, a medical glue, a medicine that makes the vein scar shut, or other materials. The coils and plugs stay in place for good.7– to 9

Stenting: opening a squeezed vein (May-Thurner syndrome)

A doctor guides a thin tube through a tiny skin nick to the squeezed vein and places a stent, a small mesh tube, to hold it open. The stent stays in for good.11

How well do they work?

Embolization. Early results are encouraging. About 8 out of 10 women have less pain after embolization. About 8 out of 10 still feel better 4 to 5 years later. In the one trial that compared embolization with a fake procedure, embolization eased pain more. More research is under way. Some may need a second treatment: up to about 1 out of 10 need a repeat procedure. It is an option to look into if you have not found relief with anything else.10, 12– to 17

Stenting. Early studies suggest a stent eases pelvic pain for women whose pain comes from a squeezed vein, and that when both vein problems are present, treating both gives more lasting relief than a stent alone. For a swollen leg, most people have less swelling after a stent. While it goes away completely in 3 to 5 out of 10 patients, 6 to 8 out of 10 patients see improvement. Pain also tends to improve more reliably than swelling.18– to 26

Side effects

Embolization. Pain in the belly or back right afterward is common. A coil can move out of place in about 2 out of 100 women. This usually causes no lasting harm. Other serious problems are possible, but rare. Pain may come back for some women.10, 12, 27, 28

Stenting. Back pain after a stent is common. A clot in the stent or the stent moving is rare, about 1 to 2 out of 100. Doctors may suggest a blood thinner during a later pregnancy.29, 30

Results vary from person to person. Talk to a doctor about whether this is right for you.

Does insurance cover pelvic vein embolization or stenting?

Long-term pelvic pain is common in women, and it has many causes. Some women try many treatments and still have no answer. For some of them, the cause is veins in the pelvis that are wide or squeezed. Embolization and stenting treat these veins without surgery. You can also consider medicines or surgery. The right choice depends on your symptoms and goals.1, 2, 31

If you have severe pain, heavy bleeding, a fever, a swollen and painful leg, or feel very sick or faint, call 911.

Your other chronic pelvic pain treatment options

Medicines

These can ease pain. No procedure is needed.

Hormone medicines

These are hormone pills or shots. Your doctor prescribes them.32

How invasive is it?
No procedure is needed.
How well does it work?
Good evidence suggests they ease pain while you take them. However, pain often comes back after you stop.32, 33
Side effects
Weight gain and bloating are common.32
Vein pills (flavonoids)

These are plant-based pills used for vein problems.34

How invasive is it?
No procedure is needed.
How well does it work?
Early studies suggest they may ease pain.34
Side effects
Side effects are few. Some people get a headache, nausea, or an upset stomach.35
Surgery

Surgery closes the wide veins or removes the organs they drain.

Removing the uterus and ovaries

A surgeon removes the uterus and both ovaries.5

How invasive is it?
This is major surgery.
How well does it work?
Early studies suggest it can ease pain. However, about 1 out of 3 women may still have some pain afterward.5
Side effects
You can no longer get pregnant. Removing both ovaries causes menopause, so doctors often give hormone replacement. It has the risks of any major surgery.5
Surgery to close the ovarian vein

A surgeon ties off and cuts the wide ovarian vein.36

How invasive is it?
This is surgery.
How well does it work?
Early studies suggest it can ease pain about as much as embolization.36
Side effects
It has the risks of any surgery.

How can veins cause pelvic pain?

Veins carry blood back to the heart. In the pelvis, veins near the ovaries and uterus can widen and let blood flow the wrong way. A large vein can also be squeezed by an artery, so blood backs up into the pelvic veins. A vein near the kidney can be squeezed too. Doctors now group these vein problems together.2

How is it found?

Doctors may use an ultrasound, a CT or MRI scan, or a dye test of the veins called a venogram.3, 4, 37

Wide or squeezed veins also show up on scans in some women who have no pain. So your doctor looks at your scans and your symptoms together.4, 5, 21, 37, 38

What should I ask my doctor?
  • Could the veins in my pelvis be part of my pain?
  • Could something else, such as endometriosis, also be part of it?
  • Which options could work for me, and which could not?
  • Do you offer pelvic vein embolization and stenting, or can you refer me to someone who does?
Common questions

Do I need treatment?

Not always. If pain limits your life, talk with your doctor about medicines first. Procedures are an option when medicines do not help.31, 39

Does the pain stop after menopause?

This is often said, but it has not been well studied.40

Can I get pregnant after treatment?

Little is known about pregnancy after embolization. Some women have become pregnant afterward. After a stent, doctors may suggest a blood thinner during pregnancy. Removing the uterus ends the chance of pregnancy.5, 13, 17, 30
