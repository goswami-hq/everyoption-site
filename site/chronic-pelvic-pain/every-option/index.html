How to read this page

It helps to know all your options before you choose. Here is what each one involves and how well it works, with the studies behind them all. If a less invasive treatment works for you, it may spare you a bigger surgery or delay it.

The pain score. Most studies on this page ask women to rate their pain from 0 to 10. Zero is no pain, and 10 is the worst pain. A drop of 1 to 2 points was the smallest change women noticed in some studies.87, 88

Different studies. Numbers from different studies cannot be set side by side. They come from different women, followed for different lengths of time. We compare two treatments only when one study tested both.

Every Option's physician performs pelvic vein embolization and stenting. They are held to the same standard of evidence as every other option here.

What is pelvic venous disease (pelvic congestion syndrome)?

Pelvic pain that lasts more than 6 months is called chronic pelvic pain. Sometimes it comes from veins in the pelvis that are wide or squeezed. Doctors call this pelvic venous disease. You may also hear the name pelvic congestion syndrome, or May-Thurner syndrome when a large vein is squeezed. It is most common in women before menopause who have been pregnant.1– to 3

Common symptoms3

  • A dull ache
  • Worse when you stand
  • Worse late in the day
  • Worse after sex
  • Worse before your period
When to see a doctor

See a doctor about pelvic pain that keeps coming back. Pelvic pain often has more than one cause, such as endometriosis, and a doctor can check for each one.4, 5

Why it is often missed

Long-term pelvic pain is common in women, and it has many causes. Many women see doctor after doctor, try treatment after treatment, and still have no answer. For some of them, the cause is the veins in the pelvis. Here is why that cause is easy to miss.1, 86

  • Doctors rarely think of the veins. A panel of experts says vein problems are rarely considered as a cause of pelvic pain.86
  • The symptoms look like other conditions. A dull ache that is worse before a period or after sex can also come from other conditions, such as endometriosis. A woman can have a vein problem and another cause of pain at the same time.3– to 5
  • Common tests can miss it. Doctors often look for the cause of pelvic pain with a small camera through the belly button (laparoscopy). During that test the belly is filled with gas and the head is tilted down. Both flatten the veins, so wide veins can look normal.3
  • Wide veins on a scan can be dismissed as normal. Wide pelvic veins also show up on scans of women who have no pain. So when a scan shows them, they may not be connected to a woman's pain.82, 83
  • Many women wait years for this diagnosis. In one study of 113 women treated for pelvic pain from a squeezed vein, the pain had lasted about 6 years on average. Women who had seen two or more other specialists first waited about 8 and a half years.44
  • Some women do not think to mention it. At one vein center, almost half of the women later treated for a squeezed vein had not brought up their pelvic pain until a doctor asked.42

If you have tried many things and still have no answer, ask your doctor whether the veins in your pelvis could be part of your pain.

Pelvic vein treatments: embolization and stenting

Two vein problems can cause this pain, and they often happen together. Veins near the ovaries and uterus can widen and let blood flow the wrong way, sometimes called pelvic congestion syndrome. A large vein in the pelvis can also be squeezed by an artery next to it, which is often called May-Thurner syndrome. Each problem has its own treatment. Both are done through a tiny skin nick, without surgery, and nothing is cut out of your body. Some women need one treatment, and some need both.43, 55, 61

Insurance coverage for these treatments varies. Ask your plan before you schedule.

Pelvic vein embolization: closing the wide veins

How well it works
Early results are encouraging About 3 out of 4 women have much less pain soon after, and for many the relief lasts for years
What it involves
A thin tube through a tiny skin nick to the wide veins. No cutting, and nothing is removed
Recovery
Usually home the same day. About a week of taking it easy

A doctor guides a thin tube through a tiny skin nick to the wide veins near the ovaries and uterus. Tiny coils, plugs, a medical glue, or a medicine then close those veins.10

You get numbing medicine at the skin, and sometimes medicine to help you relax. Most women go home the same day. Some centers keep women overnight.6, 13, 42

What is used to block the blood flow?

Embolization means blocking blood flow on purpose, from inside a blood vessel. A doctor guides a thin tube to the spot and releases a material that blocks the small blood vessels feeding it. Doctors choose the material by the size of the vessels and how long the blockage should last.26

For pelvic veins, the doctor uses tiny metal coils, small wire-mesh plugs, a medical glue, a medicine that makes the vein scar shut, or other materials. The coils and plugs stay in place for good.27– to 29

  • Tiny metal coils

    Small springs of platinum or another metal, often with soft fibers. During prostate embolization, doctors sometimes place one in a nearby artery so the beads do not reach the tissue it feeds.26, 28, 30, 31

    They stay in place for good. They are safe in an MRI scanner under set conditions; tell the scanner staff you have them.28

    Used for chronic pelvic painAlso used for enlarged prostate and hemorrhoids

  • Plugs

    A small, self-opening plug of fine wire mesh, made of nitinol (a nickel and titanium metal).29

    It stays in place for good. It is safe in an MRI scanner under set conditions; tell the scanner staff you have it.29

    Used for chronic pelvic pain

  • Medical glue (n-butyl cyanoacrylate)

    A liquid glue that the doctor injects into the vein.27

    It hardens quickly and blocks the vein.26

    Used for chronic pelvic pain

  • Vein-closing medicine (sclerosant)

    A medicine that works like a detergent, often mixed into a foam (sodium tetradecyl sulfate or polidocanol). It irritates the inside lining of the vein.32, 33

    The vein scars shut. This is not always permanent.32

    Used for chronic pelvic pain

Other materials doctors use
  • Tiny beads (microspheres)

    Round beads of plastic. One common type is an acrylic plastic with gelatin from pigs. Others are a water-holding plastic gel (polyvinyl alcohol) or a plastic gel with a thin coating.34– to 36

    They do not dissolve. They stay in place for good. They are not made of metal and do not stop you from having an MRI.34, 35, 37

    Used for fibroids, enlarged prostate, knee arthritis, and hemorrhoids

  • Small particles (polyvinyl alcohol)

    Rough, uneven flakes of a plastic.38

    They stay in place for good.38

    Used for fibroids and enlarged prostate

  • Materials that dissolve

    An antibiotic powder (imipenem and cilastatin) that forms very fine particles. It breaks down within hours to days, so blood flow can come back.39, 40

    They break down. Early studies suggest knee pain improves about the same with the antibiotic powder as with beads that stay.40, 41

    Used for knee arthritis

Some beads contain gelatin from pigs. If you avoid pork products or have had a reaction to gelatin, tell your doctor. Types without gelatin are available.34– to 36

How well does it work?

Early results are encouraging. About 3 out of 4 women have much less pain soon after embolization, and for many the relief lasts for years. In the one trial that compared embolization with a fake procedure, embolization eased pain more. More research is under way. It is an option to look into if you have not found relief with anything else.6– to 8

What the studies found

  • Embolization eased pain more than a fake procedure (a sham). Two hospitals in England randomly assigned 60 women to a dye test of the pelvic veins alone, or to the dye test plus embolization with coils. The women were not told which one they had.6
    • Pain after 12 months, on a 0-to-100 score: it fell from 43 to 15 after embolization. It stayed at 53 after the dye test alone.6
    • Quality of life improved after embolization.
    • No woman needed a hospital stay for a side effect.6
  • Pain fell after embolization in every study that measured it. A review looked at 25 studies with 2,038 women. All 18 studies that measured pain before and after found that it dropped.9
  • About 3 out of 4 women had much less pain soon afterward. A second review covered 22 studies with 1,308 women. The relief generally held or grew over time.7
  • Pain dropped by about 5 to 6 points on the 10-point pain score. The newest review combined 28 studies with 2,688 patients.10
  • For many women the relief lasted for years.
    • One hospital followed 97 women for about 4 years. Average pain fell from 7.6 to 2.9 out of 10.11
    • One center in Spain followed 520 women for about 5 years. Average pain fell from 7.6 to 0.9 out of 10, and pain came back in only 5 out of 100 women.8
  • Coils or plugs: results were about the same. A trial randomly assigned 100 women to coils or to plugs. About 9 out of 10 women were helped at 1 year with either one.12
  • Embolization eased pain more than vein pills (micronized purified flavonoid fraction, sold as Daflon). A trial at one hospital in Egypt randomly assigned 40 women to embolization with glue or to vein pills. After 12 months, average pain was 3.2 out of 10 after embolization, compared with 5.1 out of 10 on the pills. Pain came back in only 10 out of 100 women after embolization, compared with 45 out of 100 on the pills.13
  • Embolization vs. surgery to close the vein: pain fell after both.
    • A trial at one hospital randomly assigned 50 women to coil embolization or to open surgery on the ovarian vein. Pain started at 9 out of 10. It fell to 1 out of 10 after embolization and 2 out of 10 after surgery.14
    • One center looked back at 150 women who had embolization and 122 who had vein surgery. Relief came from both, but came a bit more often after vein surgery. These women were not assigned randomly.15
  • A second trial against a fake procedure is under way.16

Side effects and risks

Embolization does not cut or remove tissue. The uterus and ovaries stay, and there is no surgical wound to heal.

  • Pain in the pelvis or lower back for the first few days, sometimes with a low fever (post-embolization syndrome). It happens in about 1 out of 20 to 1 out of 5 women. It lasts a few days, and up to 2 weeks in some women. It eases with medicine and goes away.9, 17
  • Doctors usually suggest about a week of taking it easy.13, 18, 19
  • About 9 out of 100 women have a side effect of some kind, and most are minor. Examples are a bruise where the tube went in, pain after the procedure, or a mild reaction to the X-ray dye.9
  • Serious side effects are possible, but rare: about 1 out of 100. Most of these are fixed with another procedure.9
  • A coil can move out of place. This happened in fewer than 3 out of 100 women. In about 1 out of 100, a coil or plug traveled to the lung.8, 10
  • A small clot can form in a nearby vein. A center found that though some women had one, most caused no symptoms, and none traveled to the lungs.15, 20
  • Hormone levels did not change. In the study that checked, the ovaries kept working as before.11

Will I need another treatment later?

  • Pain may come back for some women. In a review of coil embolization, pain returned within 1 to 2 years in 6 to 25 out of 100 women, depending on the study.21
  • A second procedure can help. In one study of 79 women whose pain had come back, pain eased in about 2 out of 3 women after a second procedure, compared with about 1 out of 3 women who took vein pills alone.22

What experts say

Expert panels in France and Italy name embolization as the first procedure for wide pelvic veins, after scans and after other causes are checked. A U.S. expert panel says embolization and stenting should both be considered.23– to 25

Iliac vein stenting: opening a squeezed vein (May-Thurner syndrome)

How well it works
Early studies suggest It eases pelvic pain for women whose pain comes from a squeezed vein
What it involves
A thin tube through a tiny skin nick. A small mesh tube stays in the vein for good
Recovery
Usually home the same day. Some back pain for the first few days

A doctor guides a thin tube through a tiny skin nick to the squeezed vein and places a stent, a small mesh tube, to hold it open. The stent stays in for good.55

It is done as an outpatient procedure, and most people go home the same day.42, 60

How well does it work?

Early studies suggest a stent eases pelvic pain for women whose pain comes from a squeezed vein. Pain fell after a stent in every study we read. How many women were helped varied from study to study. No trial has yet compared a stent with a fake procedure for pelvic pain.43– to 45

What the studies found

  • Pelvic pain eased after a stent in 16 to 80 out of 100 women, depending on the study. A review looked at 8 studies with 1,605 patients.43
  • In the longest study, about 8 out of 10 women had no pelvic pain years later. One practice in Australia followed 113 women for about 7 years after a stent. Average pain fell from 6.7 to 0.8 out of 10.44, 46
  • At a U.S. vein center, pain fell and stayed lower for a year. The center placed a stent in 141 women who had both a squeezed vein and wide veins. Average pain fell from 6.4 to about 2 out of 10 at 12 months.45
  • When the narrowing clearly slowed blood flow, about 7 out of 10 women had relief after a stent alone. This was one study of 49 women. The women who still had pain went on to have embolization, and it eased their pain.47
  • The stronger stent studies are about leg symptoms. One trial randomly assigned people with leg vein disease to a stent or to medical care. Leg pain fell from 8 to 2.5 out of 10 with a stent, compared with 8 to 7 out of 10 without one. No trial like this has been done for pelvic pain.48

Side effects and risks

A stent does not cut or remove tissue. It is a permanent implant, so it needs follow-up over the years.

  • Expect some back pain for the first few days. Your doctor gives you pain medicine in case you need it. The pain eases with time.
  • Serious side effects are rare. Serious bleeding or a clot in the lungs happened in about 1 out of 100 people or fewer.49
  • A clot in the stent is rare: about 1 out of 100 people or fewer.43, 50
  • A stent moving out of place is very rare: about 2 out of 1,000 people. It can be serious when it happens.51
  • Two vein stents were recalled in 2021: the Vici and the Venovo. Ask your doctor which stent they use.52
  • Most stents stay open. About 97 to 99 out of 100 were still open at 1 year, and about 94 out of 100 at 3 years. These studies were in people treated for leg symptoms.49, 53
  • You may take a blood thinner for a few months.54
  • Long-term results in young women are still being studied. A stent placed at 30 has to last for decades, and studies that long are not yet available.45, 55

Who it is for

A stent is not for everyone. It is for a select group of women whose pain comes from a squeezed vein. This group is often missed.

  • A squeezed vein on a scan is not enough. In one study of CT scans, about 1 out of 4 people with no vein symptoms had this vein squeezed by more than half. Your doctor looks at your symptoms and your scans together.56
  • The narrowing can change with your position. In a study of 100 women whose vein was squeezed by more than half while lying down, only 32 still had that narrowing when standing.57
  • Doctors confirm the narrowing from inside the vein. Other causes of pain are checked first. Then the narrowing is measured during the procedure, often with a tiny ultrasound probe inside the vein.54, 55
  • Some women with a squeezed vein do well with embolization alone. In one study of 102 women who had embolization and no stent, pain was controlled in about 8 out of 10, whether or not they also had a squeezed vein.58

Will I need another treatment later?

  • About 1 out of 10 women needed another procedure within a few years. For some it was embolization of the pelvic veins. For others it was a second procedure on the stent.42, 45

What experts say

U.S. vein experts say a stent may help some women whose pelvic pain comes from a squeezed vein and limits their life. They advise against a stent for people with few or no symptoms, or when the narrowing is mild.55, 59

When both vein problems are present

Many women have both wide veins and a squeezed vein. Which treatment should come first, and who needs both, is still being researched.

  • In one small trial, treating both gave more lasting relief than a stent alone. A hospital in Egypt randomly assigned 20 women with both problems to a stent alone or to a stent plus embolization. Relief lasted in 10 out of 10 women after both treatments, compared with 5 out of 10 women after a stent alone.61
  • In a larger study, more women improved when embolization was added. Among 184 women, at least half the pain was gone at 12 months in 55 out of 100 after a stent alone, compared with 74 out of 100 after a stent and embolization together. The women were not assigned randomly.62
  • Treating both may bring relief sooner. At one U.S. vein center, pain reached its lowest point by 1 month after both treatments, compared with 3 months after either one alone.54
  • Doctors differ on the order. Some treat the squeezed vein first and add embolization only if pain stays. Others start with embolization, so that a woman may not need a permanent implant.47, 54, 58

Medicines compared

Hormone medicines

How well it works
Good evidence suggests They ease pain while you take them. Pain often comes back after you stop
What it involves
Pills or shots
Recovery
None

These are hormone pills or shots. Your doctor prescribes them.63

How well does it work?

Good evidence suggests hormone medicines ease pain while you take them. However, pain often comes back after you stop.63, 64

What the studies found

  • Hormone pills eased pain more than a dummy pill (a placebo). A trial randomly assigned 84 women to a hormone pill (medroxyprogesterone) or a dummy pill for 4 months. At least half the pain was gone in 73 out of 100 women on the hormone pill, compared with 33 out of 100 women on the dummy pill.64
  • The benefit faded after the pills were stopped. In the same trial, 9 months after stopping, pain was about the same in both groups.64
  • A hormone-lowering shot helped more than the pills in one small trial. A trial randomly assigned 47 women to a monthly shot (goserelin) or to hormone pills for 6 months. A year later, pain relief was greater after the shot.65

Side effects and risks

  • Weight gain and bloating are common with hormone pills.63
  • Hot flashes, headaches and sweating are common with the hormone-lowering shot. Each affects more than 1 out of 5 women who take it.66
  • Do not take hormone pills while trying for a pregnancy.63

Vein pills (flavonoids)

How well it works
Early studies suggest They may ease pain
What it involves
A daily pill
Recovery
None

These are plant-based pills used for vein problems.67

How well does it work?

Early studies suggest they may ease pain. The studies were small and lasted about 2 months.67, 68

What the studies found

  • Pain eased in the studies so far. A review found 11 studies with 748 women. Pain improved in each, but only 3 were small trials that assigned women randomly.67
  • Vein pills eased pain more than a dummy pill in one trial. In 83 women followed for 2 months, pain and quality of life improved more with the vein pill. The pill's maker paid for the study.68
  • Embolization eased pain more than vein pills. In the trial of 40 women, average pain at 12 months was 5.1 out of 10 on the pills, compared with 3.2 out of 10 after embolization.13

Side effects and risks

  • Side effects are few. In a trial of 150 women, only about 7 out of 100 had a headache, nausea, stomach pain, or diarrhea. None was serious.69

Is waiting safe?

If pain limits your life, talk with your doctor about medicines first. Procedures are an option when medicines do not help. We found no study showing that waiting makes vein-related pelvic pain harder to treat later.23, 24

Surgery compared

Hysterectomy with removal of both ovaries

How well it works
Early studies suggest It can ease pain. About 1 out of 3 women may still have some pain afterward
What it involves
Major surgery. It ends pregnancy and causes menopause
Recovery
2 to 6 weeks

A surgeon removes the uterus and both ovaries. This is major surgery.5

How well does it work?

Early studies suggest it can ease pain. However, about 1 out of 3 women may still have some pain afterward.5

What the studies found

  • Most women had far less pain a year later. One hospital followed 36 women with pelvic vein pain. A year after surgery, the typical pain score had fallen from 10 to 0 out of 10. However, 12 of the 36 women still had some pain.5
  • Hysterectomy vs. embolization: one small study compared them. A study in South Korea of 106 women reported that pain fell more after embolization than after hysterectomy. The study had weaknesses, so this result is uncertain.70, 71
  • After hysterectomy for long-term pelvic pain of any cause, about 1 out of 4 women still had some pain a year later. A U.S. study followed 308 women. Other studies found anywhere from 12 to 68 out of 100.72, 73
  • No trial has tested hysterectomy for this pain.74

Side effects and risks

No study gives side-effect rates for this surgery when it is done for pelvic vein pain. The numbers here are for hysterectomy done for any noncancer reason.

  • You can no longer get pregnant.5
  • Removing both ovaries causes menopause right away. Doctors often give hormone replacement.5
  • A major complication happens in about 4 to 5 out of 100 women. Examples are an injury to the bowel or bladder tubes, or a return to the operating room. This is from a study of 194,570 women in England.75
  • A blood clot in the leg or lungs is rare: about 2 out of 1,000 women.76
  • Recovery takes 2 to 6 weeks. It is shorter after surgery through the vagina or through small cuts than after a larger cut in the belly.77, 78
  • Early menopause is linked to a somewhat higher chance of heart disease later in life. A review combined 18 studies. Women who had both ovaries removed before menopause were more likely to develop heart disease than women who kept them.79

Surgery to close the ovarian vein

How well it works
Early studies suggest It can ease pain about as much as embolization
What it involves
Surgery to tie off and cut the wide vein
Recovery
Not reported in the studies we read

A surgeon ties off and cuts the wide ovarian vein.14

How well does it work?

Early studies suggest it can ease pain about as much as embolization.14, 15

What the studies found

  • Surgery vs. embolization: pain fell after both.
    • In the trial of 50 women, pain started at 9 out of 10. It fell to 2 out of 10 after surgery and 1 out of 10 after embolization.14
    • At one center that looked back at 122 women who had vein surgery and 150 who had embolization, relief came from both, but came a bit more often after vein surgery.15

Side effects and risks

  • This is surgery, with a cut in the belly.14
  • Side effects in the first month can happen. In the one study that reported them, most were a small clot in a nearby vein, found on a scan.15

Pregnancy after each treatment

  • Embolization. Little is known. Some women have become pregnant afterward, and the reviews found no sign of harm to periods or the ovaries.7, 11
  • Stenting. Women have had healthy pregnancies with a stent in place. In two small reports, 27 women had 33 pregnancies, and no stent clotted. Doctors may suggest a blood thinner during pregnancy.46, 80, 81
  • Hormone medicines. Hormone pills are not taken while trying for a pregnancy.63
  • Hysterectomy. It ends the chance of pregnancy.5

How is it found?

Doctors may use an ultrasound, a CT or MRI scan, or a dye test of the veins called a venogram.3, 82

Wide or squeezed veins also show up on scans in some women who have no pain. So your doctor looks at your scans and your symptoms together.56, 82, 83

  • Wide veins are common on scans. In one study of 2,384 CT scans of women, about 1 out of 8 showed wide ovarian veins. About 1 out of 5 of those women had pelvic pain on record.82
  • A detailed ultrasound can come close to the dye test. In one study, an ultrasound that scored several vein features together matched the dye test well.84

Does the pain stop after menopause?

This is often said, but it has not been well studied.85
