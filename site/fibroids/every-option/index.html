What are fibroids?

Fibroids are muscle growths in or on the wall of the uterus. They are very common. By age 50, about 2 to 8 out of 10 women have them.5

Common symptoms5, 6

  • Heavy or long periods
  • Tiredness from blood loss
  • Pelvic pain or pressure
  • Frequent urination
  • Pain during sex
When to see a doctor

See a doctor if you have any of these, or if you soak through a pad or tampon every hour for 2 to 3 hours. A doctor can check what is causing your symptoms and go over your options.7

Fibroid medicines compared

These treat symptoms like heavy bleeding and pain. No procedure is needed.

Medicines without hormones

How well it works
Ease bleeding and cramps; do not treat the fibroids
What it involves
Pills on period days
Recovery
None
Keeps uterus?
Yes
Treats the fibroids?
No. Eases bleeding and cramps

You take tranexamic acid only on the days of your period. Pain relievers and iron can help with cramps and tiredness from blood loss.5, 6, 8

How well does it work?

These medicines ease bleeding and cramps. They do not touch the fibroids, so symptoms come back when you stop.6, 8

Side effects and risks

Blood clots from tranexamic acid are possible, but rare.8

Hormone medicines

How well it works
Strong evidence shows Greatly reduce bleeding; symptoms often return after stopping
What it involves
Pills or shots
Recovery
None
Keeps uterus?
Yes
Treats the fibroids?
Eases symptoms, which often return after stopping

This group includes birth control pills or shots, daily pills that lower hormones, and short-term shots before surgery.6, 15

How well does it work?

Strong evidence shows daily hormone-lowering pills greatly reduce bleeding. In a drug-maker-funded trial, bleeding fell greatly for about 7 out of 10 women. Symptoms often come back after stopping.5, 9

The studies

  • The strong evidence is for bleeding over the first months. In four large trials of two medicines (relugolix and elagolix), women were assigned by chance to the medicine or to a placebo (a dummy pill with no active ingredient) and followed over 6 months.9, 10
  • Some women on the dummy pill also improved. In those four trials, about 1 to 2 out of 10 women on the dummy pill met the same bleeding goal (8.7% to 19%). The medicine's real effect is the gap between the two groups.9, 10
  • Use beyond six months rests mostly on extension studies. In these, every woman knew she was taking the medicine. One study assigned some women by chance to switch to a dummy pill. Most of those who stopped the medicine saw heavy bleeding return.11, 12

Side effects and risks

Hot flashes can happen. Some of these medicines weaken bones, so how long you can take them is limited. They are not for women who have had blood clots.13– to 15

Embolization vs. other less invasive treatments

These treat fibroids or bleeding without major surgery. Your uterus stays in place. Before you agree to surgery, ask about these.

Fibroid embolization (UFE)

How well it works
Good evidence suggests Most women feel much better
What it involves
Tiny skin nick
Recovery
About 1 week
Keeps uterus?
Yes
Treats the fibroids?
Yes. Shrinks them

An interventional radiologist passes a thin tube through a tiny skin nick to the arteries that feed the fibroids. Tiny beads or particles block that blood flow, and the fibroids shrink. You keep your uterus. There is no cut in the belly, so there is no belly scar where a hernia can form. Most women go home the same day. Recovery takes about 1 week, compared with 2 to 6 weeks after major surgery.1– to 4, 6

What is used to block the blood flow?

Embolization means blocking blood flow on purpose, from inside a blood vessel. A doctor guides a thin tube to the spot and releases a material that blocks the small blood vessels feeding it. Doctors choose the material by the size of the vessels and how long the blockage should last.28

  • Tiny beads (microspheres)

    Round beads of plastic. One common type is an acrylic plastic with gelatin from pigs. Others are a water-holding plastic gel (polyvinyl alcohol) or a plastic gel with a thin coating.29– to 31

    They do not dissolve. They stay in place for good. They are not made of metal and do not stop you from having an MRI.29, 30, 32

    Used for fibroidsAlso used for enlarged prostate, knee arthritis, and hemorrhoids

  • Small particles (polyvinyl alcohol)

    Rough, uneven flakes of a plastic.33

    They stay in place for good.33

    Used for fibroidsAlso used for enlarged prostate

Other materials doctors use
  • Tiny metal coils

    Small springs of platinum or another metal, often with soft fibers. During prostate embolization, doctors sometimes place one in a nearby artery so the beads do not reach the tissue it feeds.28, 34– to 36

    They stay in place for good. They are safe in an MRI scanner under set conditions; tell the scanner staff you have them.34

    Used for enlarged prostate, hemorrhoids, and chronic pelvic pain

  • Plugs

    A small, self-opening plug of fine wire mesh, made of nitinol (a nickel and titanium metal).37

    It stays in place for good. It is safe in an MRI scanner under set conditions; tell the scanner staff you have it.37

    Used for chronic pelvic pain

  • Medical glue (n-butyl cyanoacrylate)

    A liquid glue that the doctor injects into the vein.38

    It hardens quickly and blocks the vein.28

    Used for chronic pelvic pain

  • Vein-closing medicine (sclerosant)

    A medicine that works like a detergent, often mixed into a foam (sodium tetradecyl sulfate or polidocanol). It irritates the inside lining of the vein.39, 40

    The vein scars shut. This is not always permanent.39

    Used for chronic pelvic pain

  • Materials that dissolve

    An antibiotic powder (imipenem and cilastatin) that forms very fine particles. It breaks down within hours to days, so blood flow can come back.41, 42

    They break down. Early studies suggest knee pain improves about the same with the antibiotic powder as with beads that stay.42, 43

    Used for knee arthritis

Some beads contain gelatin from pigs. If you avoid pork products or have had a reaction to gelatin, tell your doctor. Types without gelatin are available.29– to 31

How well does it work?

Good evidence suggests most women feel much better: almost 9 out of 10 women see their symptoms improve, and about 7 out of 10 women still feel better five years later. Most feel just as satisfied as women who had surgery (hysterectomy or myomectomy). The tradeoff is that the uterus stays, so about 1 out of 4 may need another procedure down the line. Even so, after ten years, about 2 out of 3 women avoided a hysterectomy entirely.18– to 23

The studies

  • Most women's symptoms improve. A German health-information institute puts symptom improvement at about 80 to 90 out of 100 women.18
  • Satisfaction: no clear difference from surgery. A Cochrane review combined 7 trials (793 women) that assigned women by chance to embolization or to surgery (hysterectomy or myomectomy). It found no clear difference in satisfaction between embolization and surgery up to 2 years (6 trials, 640 women). There was no clear difference at 5 years either.20
  • Quality of life: no clear lasting difference from myomectomy. A trial assigned 254 women at 29 UK hospitals by chance to myomectomy or to embolization. At 2 years, fibroid quality-of-life scores out of 100 were 84.6 after myomectomy and 80.0 after embolization. After allowing for differences between the women, myomectomy scored 8.0 points higher. At 4 years, myomectomy was 5.0 points higher, a gap that could be due to chance.22, 24
  • Taken together: no clear difference in satisfaction. On quality of life, myomectomy scored somewhat higher at first, and later the gap could be due to chance.20, 22, 24

Side effects and risks

Strong cramping pain, like very bad period cramps, is common in the first few days. It often comes with nausea and a low fever. Doctors call this post-embolization syndrome. It usually eases within a week. Early menopause is uncommon: less than 5 out of 100 women go through menopause early. Overall, serious problems are no more common than after surgery.1, 20, 25, 26

What experts say

A radiology expert panel rates embolization usually appropriate or possibly appropriate across the fibroid situations it reviewed. Before you agree to surgery, ask about it.27

Radiofrequency ablation

How well it works
Results vary: about 1 out of 11 women needed surgery within 3 years in one study, about 2 out of 5 within 2 years in another
What it involves
Small cuts, or no cuts through the vagina
Recovery
Most go home the same day
Keeps uterus?
Yes
Treats the fibroids?
Yes. Heat destroys the fibroid

Heat from a small needle destroys the fibroid. The doctor uses small cuts in the belly, or goes through the vagina with no cuts. Most women go home the same day.6, 44, 48

How well does it work?

Results vary from study to study: about 1 out of 11 women needed surgery within 3 years in one study, and about 2 out of 5 within 2 years in another. The first study was funded by the device maker.44, 45

The studies

  • These two figures differ mainly in what they count.
    • The first study counted only surgery for heavy bleeding.44
    • The second counted any surgery, most often removing other fibroids through the cervix. It looked back at one center's records.45
  • Across several studies, some women needed another procedure. Across studies that followed women forward from treatment, about 1 out of 9 women had another procedure for fibroid symptoms by 3 years (11.5%).46
  • Compared with myomectomy, more women needed a second surgery. A Dutch study followed women after the treatment they chose; treatments were not assigned by chance. Within 18 months, about 1 out of 4 women (27.3%) needed another surgery after the no-cut radiofrequency treatment through the vagina, compared with about 1 out of 16 (6.3%) after myomectomy.47

Side effects and risks

Pain and cramps can happen. Serious problems are possible, but were rare in studies.44, 45

Focused ultrasound

What it involves
No cut, in an MRI scanner

Keeps uterus?
Yes
Treats the fibroids?
Yes, for some fibroids

Sound waves, guided by an MRI scanner, heat and shrink the fibroid. There is no cut, and most women recover quickly.25

How well does it work?

Early studies suggest it eases symptoms. It fits only some fibroids, and some women need more treatment later. Not all insurance covers it, so check first.25, 49, 50

The studies

  • It eased symptoms more than a fake treatment in one very small trial. A small pilot trial assigned 20 women by chance to focused ultrasound or to a placebo (a fake treatment). By 12 weeks, symptoms improved more after focused ultrasound than after the fake treatment (31 versus 13 points on a symptom score). At 4 weeks, the difference could be due to chance.51
  • Repeat treatment is common and rises with time. In one group of 138 women, 19% had more treatment by 3 years and 23% by 4 years. In another group of 162 women, 59% had more treatment by 5 years.52, 53
  • More women needed another procedure than after embolization. A small trial treated 83 women with focused ultrasound or embolization; some of them were assigned by chance. Another fibroid procedure followed for 13 of 43 (30%) after focused ultrasound, compared with 5 of 40 (12.5%) after embolization.54

Side effects and risks

Belly pain can happen. Skin burns or bruises can happen and usually heal in a week or two.25

What it involves
No cuts
Recovery
1 to 2 days
Keeps uterus?
Yes
Treats the fibroids?
No. Treats the lining

Heat or cold destroys the lining of the uterus to lighten periods. There are no cuts. Most women go home the same day and recover in 1 to 2 days.58

How well does it work?

It treats the lining, not the fibroids, so they stay in place. The American College of Radiology's expert panel rates it usually not appropriate for fibroids. In studies of heavy bleeding, about 1 out of 8 women had a hysterectomy within 5 years.6, 27, 55

The studies

That hysterectomy figure comes from women with heavy bleeding in general, not women with fibroids. Studies of women with fibroids come from health records. None is a trial.55

  • Women with fibroids more often had a hysterectomy later. In a Finnish national registry, women with fibroids were almost twice as likely to have a hysterectomy after ablation, after allowing for other differences.56
  • Some women with fibroids needed another procedure after ablation. In a large U.S. health system study of women treated for fibroids, 35.5% needed another procedure within 7 years of ablation.57

Side effects and risks

Cramps and discharge can last a while. It is not for anyone who may want a future pregnancy.58, 59

Myomectomy vs. hysterectomy, and other surgery

Surgery removes the fibroids or the whole uterus.

A thin camera and small tools go through the cervix and remove fibroids growing inside the uterus. There are no cuts, and recovery takes a few days.3, 6, 60

How well does it work?

Early studies suggest it helps. It only works for fibroids inside the uterus. New fibroids can form later.5, 6

Side effects and risks

Cramps and light bleeding can last a few days. Heavy bleeding, infection, or a tear in the uterus are possible, but rare.60

What it involves
Surgery: larger cut or a few small cuts
Recovery
2 to 6 weeks
Keeps uterus?
Yes
Treats the fibroids?
Yes. Removes those the surgeon can see

Surgery removes the fibroids and leaves the uterus. It takes a larger cut in the belly or a few small cuts. Recovery takes 2 to 6 weeks.3, 4, 6

How well does it work?

Good evidence suggests women's quality of life improves. But small or deep fibroids can be missed, and the more you have, the harder it is to remove them all. New ones can also grow, and about 1 out of 8 women may need another procedure down the line.3, 6, 22, 24, 61, 62

The studies

  • Repeat procedures rise over time. About 1 out of 8 women had another procedure by 4 to 5 years (13% at 4 years in one trial; 12.2% at 5 years across several studies combined). About 1 out of 5 had one by 7 years (20.6% in a large U.S. health system study).22, 57, 63
  • A second combined analysis found a higher rate. It found about 1 out of 5 women had another procedure by 5 years after myomectomy (19%), more than the first combined figure. In the same analysis, the figure was higher after embolization (21%) and after focused ultrasound (49%).64

Side effects and risks

Infection, blood clots, and harm to nearby organs are possible, but not common. Bleeding is the main risk: about 1 out of 5 women need a blood transfusion after open surgery, and about 1 out of 20 after surgery through small cuts, in a review of 9 studies. Scar tissue (adhesions) forms inside the belly in most women. It often causes no symptoms, but it can cause pain or block the bowel. After open surgery, up to 1 out of 5 people get a hernia at the scar.3, 65– to 68

Hysterectomy

How well it works
The only treatment that keeps fibroids from returning; periods stop
What it involves
Surgery: removes the uterus
Recovery
2 to 6 weeks
Keeps uterus?
No
Treats the fibroids?
Yes. They cannot return

Surgery removes the whole uterus, through the vagina, small cuts, or a larger cut in the belly. Recovery takes 2 to 6 weeks.3, 4

How well does it work?

It is the only treatment that keeps fibroids from returning. Periods stop, so the heavy bleeding from fibroids ends. Most women feel much better afterward and stay satisfied years later. It also ends the chance of pregnancy.3, 5, 23, 69

The studies

  • Compared with embolization: no clear difference in quality of life. A trial in the Netherlands assigned 177 women by chance to embolization or to hysterectomy. Quality of life improved after both, with no clear difference between them at 5 or 10 years. At 10 years, 87% of women were satisfied after hysterectomy and 78% after embolization. Combined with other trials, satisfaction did not clearly differ between embolization and surgery.20, 23, 70
  • More women needed more treatment after embolization, but most kept their uterus. By 10 years in the Dutch trial, 35% of women treated with embolization (28 of 81) went on to have a hysterectomy, so about 2 out of 3 kept their uterus. In a UK trial, 32% needed another procedure within 5 years after embolization, compared with 4% after surgery, which was mostly hysterectomy.21, 23
  • Most women go home the same day after embolization. At one U.S. hospital, 9 out of 10 women who had a nerve block for pain during embolization went home the same day. Without the block, about half did. Some women stay one night for pain control. Embolization is also done in outpatient offices, with no hospital stay at all. In U.S. health records from 2016 to 2019, the stay was 1 day after embolization and 5 days after hysterectomy. Older trials kept women in the hospital longer. In the Dutch trial, women spent an average of 2.5 days in the hospital after embolization and 5.1 days after hysterectomy. That trial ran in the early 2000s, and stays after embolization are shorter now. Surgery through small cuts or the vagina usually means a shorter stay than a larger cut in the belly.71– to 75
  • How the surgery is done changes recovery. A Cochrane review of 63 trials (6,811 women) compared ways of doing the surgery. Women got back to normal activities about 11 days sooner after surgery through the vagina, and about 13 days sooner after surgery through small cuts, than after a larger cut in the belly. Surgery through small cuts had about twice as many injuries to the bladder or the tubes that carry urine. The review's example: if about 2 out of 1,000 women have an injury to a tube from the kidney after a larger cut, the figure through small cuts would be between 2 out of 1,000 and 2 out of 100.75
  • Most women improve, but not all. In a Maryland study, 1,299 women had a hysterectomy for noncancer reasons (not only fibroids). Symptoms eased and quality of life rose over 2 years. About 1 out of 12 (8%) still had as many severe symptoms 1 and 2 years later.69

Side effects and risks

Bleeding, infection, blood clots, and harm to nearby organs can happen. In the Dutch trial, serious problems were not clearly more common after either treatment (2.7% after hysterectomy, 4.9% after embolization). In the 6 weeks after leaving the hospital, minor problems were less common after hysterectomy (40% compared with 58%), and no one went back into the hospital, compared with 11% after embolization.4, 74

Menopause begins right away if both ovaries are also removed. Scar tissue can make organs stick together. A blocked bowel from scar tissue is possible, but rare. After open surgery, up to 1 out of 5 people get a hernia at the scar.4, 66– to 68

Longer-term effects are less certain, because they come from studies that followed women after surgery, not from trials.

When the ovaries are kept, studies disagree on whether menopause comes sooner. In one U.S. study, the ovaries stopped working within 4 years in 14.8% of women after hysterectomy, compared with 8.0% of similar women who did not have one. A later U.S. study that tracked an ovarian hormone found no faster decline after hysterectomy.76, 77

In a Swedish national study of 165,260 women who had a hysterectomy, later surgery for leaking urine when coughing or exercising (stress incontinence) was more than twice as common as in women who did not have one.78

In a Minnesota study that followed 2,094 women for about 22 years after a hysterectomy that kept the ovaries, high blood pressure and heart disease were somewhat more common than in women who did not have one, more so for women 35 or younger. A study like this cannot show that the surgery caused it.79
