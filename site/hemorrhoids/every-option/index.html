How to read this page

This page goes over the evidence behind the short guide. Each treatment starts with the conclusion and how sure we are based on studies, then the actual studies, the harms, and what is not yet known.

Numbers from different studies are not head-to-head comparisons. They come from different people, follow-up times, and outcome definitions, so this page never lines them up as if they did.

When a study gives a range after a number, the true result most likely falls inside that range. A wide range means the study could not pin the result down.

Hemorrhoid grades run from the lowest (which bleed but do not bulge) to the highest (which bulge out and stay out).

Every Option's physician performs hemorrhoid artery embolization. It is held to the same standard of evidence as every other option here.

What are hemorrhoids?

Hemorrhoids are swollen veins inside the lower rectum (the last part of the bowel) or under the skin around the anus. Hemorrhoids that are inside the rectum can bleed or bulge out and are usually painless. Outside ones can itch, ache, or form a tender lump.6

Common symptoms6

  • Bright red blood on the toilet paper
  • A lump that bulges out
  • Itching
  • Aching or a tender lump
When to see a doctor

Rectal bleeding needs a check with a doctor first. Bleeding can come from other problems, such as colon cancer or bowel inflammation, so see a doctor about any bleeding. Sudden, severe pain can mean a blood clot in a hemorrhoid or a small tear (fissure), and needs a doctor soon.6, 7

Bleeding after banding: how the paths compare

For bleeding that keeps coming back after office care, the choice is usually more office treatment, embolization, or surgery.1– to 5, 30, 33

  • Surgery has the strongest record for lasting results and the hardest recovery.
  • Embolization: early studies suggest it spares most of the painful recovery. It treats bleeding, but its benefit fades for some within a year, and it is not known to fix bulging hemorrhoids.
  • More office treatment: banding can be repeated, and it works well for many people.
  • No trial has compared embolization with banding yet.

Fiber, creams, pills, and home care compared

Most people start here. No procedure is needed.

Fiber and home care

How well it works
Good evidence suggests Eases symptoms and lowers the chance of bleeding
What it involves
More fiber, less straining
Recovery
None
Hemorrhoids that bulge out
Not shown to help

Fiber from food or a supplement softens stool, and the advice is to avoid straining or sitting a long time on the toilet. The AGA review calls this a reasonable first step.7

Bottom line

Good evidence suggests fiber eases symptoms and lowers the chance of bleeding. It may not help much with bulging, pain, or itching.8

What the studies found

7 trials of 378 people compared fiber with no fiber:8

  • Fewer symptoms: people taking fiber were about half as likely to still have symptoms as people not taking it (risk ratio 0.53; range 0.38 to 0.73).8
  • Less bleeding: they were also about half as likely to still bleed (risk ratio 0.50; range 0.28 to 0.89).8
  • Bulging, pain, and itching: no clear effect. The ranges were wide and included no difference at all.
  • The trials are older and of moderate quality.

Harms

Mild side effects were more common with fiber in the trials, but the difference could be chance.8

What experts say

The AGA lists fiber and changes in toilet habits as first-line care.7

What is not known

Whether fiber stops hemorrhoids from coming back has not been well tested.8

Creams and warm baths

How well it works
Early studies suggest Creams and warm baths may ease symptoms for a while; not shown to fix hemorrhoids.
What it involves
Numbing, witch hazel, or steroid creams and wipes; sitting in a few inches of warm water (a sitz bath)
Recovery
None
Hemorrhoids that bulge out
Not shown to help

These treat symptoms, not the hemorrhoid itself. That is often enough: many people mainly need the bleeding, itching, or soreness to settle, and doctors often recommend them for that. The AGA says warm baths are often advised and creams can be considered.7

Bottom line

Many people find they ease symptoms for a while, but there is little proof they help in the long run. Small trials of single creams suggest short-term relief. Warm baths may also provide some symptomatic relief. Neither has been shown to make hemorrhoids go away or stop them coming back.7, 9– to 13

What the studies found

  • Creams eased symptoms over a few weeks in small trials. Each tested one product against a cream with no active ingredient (a placebo):
    • A drug-free cream, in 60 people with lower-grade hemorrhoids: every symptom improved more than with placebo after 1 and 2 weeks.9
    • A boric-acid gel, in 206 people over 4 weeks: less itching, pain, and feeling of a lump. Bleeding with bowel movements: 42% with the gel vs 67% with placebo.10
    • An herbal cream, in 52 people over 3 weeks: less irritation, bleeding, pain, and swelling; itching was no different.11
  • Numbing ointment helps briefly after banding. In 99 people, lidocaine ointment eased pain in the first hour more than placebo; the effect was short-lived.12
  • Warm baths relax the anal muscle for a while. In 28 people with painful anal problems (10 with hemorrhoids) and 18 healthy volunteers, sitting in warm water relaxed the inner anal muscle and eased pain. The muscle tightened again 25 to 70 minutes later.13
  • These trials are small and short, and each tested a single product. Results may not carry over to other creams (our inference).

Harms

Steroid creams should not be used for more than 2 weeks at a time. Use warm, not hot, water: a burn from a hot sitz bath has been reported.7, 14

What experts say

The AGA says warm baths are often advised to ease symptoms, with limited data, and that creams can be considered, with little data to support them.7

What is not known

Which creams work best, and whether any of them help beyond a few weeks.7

Flavonoid pills

How well it works
Early studies suggest Ease bleeding and itching; reviews disagree on pain
What it involves
Daily pills
Recovery
None
Hemorrhoids that bulge out
Not stated in our sources

Flavonoids are plant compounds taken as pills. They are one kind of vein medicine (phlebotonic); the group also includes some synthetic drugs. Like creams, they ease symptoms rather than remove the hemorrhoid.15, 16

Bottom line

Early studies suggest flavonoid pills ease bleeding and itching; the two large reviews disagree on pain. There are many trials, but they are of limited quality.15, 16

What the studies found

14 trials of 1,514 people compared flavonoids with a placebo or no treatment:15

  • Fewer people still had symptoms: people taking flavonoids were less than half as likely to still have symptoms (risk ratio 0.42; range 0.28 to 0.61).15
  • Less bleeding: about a third as likely to still bleed (risk ratio 0.33; range 0.19 to 0.57).15
  • Less pain and itching: about a third as likely to still have pain (risk ratio 0.35) and about two thirds as likely to still itch (risk ratio 0.65).15
  • Fewer returns: about half as likely to have symptoms come back (risk ratio 0.53; range 0.41 to 0.69).15
  • A Cochrane review agreed: in 20 trials of 2,344 people, vein pills (phlebotonics, which include flavonoids and some synthetic drugs) meant less bleeding, itching, and leakage than a comparison treatment, and more people improved overall. Unlike the first review, it found no clear effect on pain.16
  • The trials varied a lot, their quality was limited, and the authors worried that trials with poor results may never have been published.15

Harms

The Cochrane review found few safety concerns in the trials. In pregnancy, a Cochrane review of 2 trials (150 women) found a related pill eased symptoms, but said it should not be recommended until its safety is shown.16, 17

What is not known

How they compare with office treatments such as banding and how long the benefit lasts after stopping (our reading; our sources do not test these).15

Do hemorrhoids get better without a procedure?

One trial, from 1989, tested this. About 186 people with a first episode of grade 2 hemorrhoids were assigned by chance to no treatment or to banding. They were followed for 4 years.18

  • Banding resolved more people's first episodes. Symptoms did not clear in about 1 out of 3 people left untreated (34%), against about 1 out of 16 who were banded (6%).18
  • Some got better on their own. 1 out of 4 people left untreated (25%) had no further episode in 4 years.18
  • Hemorrhoids often came back. Among people whose first episode resolved, symptoms returned in about 6 out of 10 left untreated (61%), against about 3 out of 10 after banding (33%).18

This data is old and small. It is a reason to start simple, not a promise.18

Hemorrhoid embolization vs. banding and more

These need no surgery. Banding is usually tried first, in the office. Embolization is an option when bleeding keeps coming back.

Hemorrhoid artery embolization (HAE)

How well it works
Early results are encouraging Most bleed less; bleeding returns for some within a year
What it involves
A thin tube through a tiny skin nick in the wrist or groin
Recovery
Most go home the same day; some may experience a few days of pressure or urge to go
Hemorrhoids that bulge out
Not known to help

A catheter enters the wrist or groin artery, under local numbing and moderate sedation, and is guided to the superior rectal arteries that feed the hemorrhoids. Coils, particles, or both block them; the middle rectal arteries are sometimes treated too. No embolic agent is proven best.3, 5, 23

What is used to block the blood flow?

Embolization means blocking blood flow on purpose, from inside a blood vessel. A doctor guides a thin tube to the spot and releases a material that blocks the small blood vessels feeding it. Doctors choose the material by the size of the vessels and how long the blockage should last.35

  • Tiny metal coils

    Small springs of platinum or another metal, often with soft fibers. During prostate embolization, doctors sometimes place one in a nearby artery so the beads do not reach the tissue it feeds.35– to 38

    They stay in place for good. They are safe in an MRI scanner under set conditions; tell the scanner staff you have them.36

    Used for hemorrhoidsAlso used for chronic pelvic pain

  • Tiny beads (microspheres)

    Round beads of plastic. One common type is an acrylic plastic with gelatin from pigs. Others are a water-holding plastic gel (polyvinyl alcohol) or a plastic gel with a thin coating.39– to 41

    They do not dissolve. They stay in place for good. They are not made of metal and do not stop you from having an MRI.39, 40, 42

    Used for hemorrhoidsAlso used for fibroids, enlarged prostate, and knee arthritis

Other materials doctors use
  • Small particles (polyvinyl alcohol)

    Rough, uneven flakes of a plastic.43

    They stay in place for good.43

    Used for fibroids and enlarged prostate

  • Plugs

    A small, self-opening plug of fine wire mesh, made of nitinol (a nickel and titanium metal).44

    It stays in place for good. It is safe in an MRI scanner under set conditions; tell the scanner staff you have it.44

    Used for chronic pelvic pain

  • Medical glue (n-butyl cyanoacrylate)

    A liquid glue that the doctor injects into the vein.45

    It hardens quickly and blocks the vein.35

    Used for chronic pelvic pain

  • Vein-closing medicine (sclerosant)

    A medicine that works like a detergent, often mixed into a foam (sodium tetradecyl sulfate or polidocanol). It irritates the inside lining of the vein.46, 47

    The vein scars shut. This is not always permanent.46

    Used for chronic pelvic pain

  • Materials that dissolve

    Materials made to break down within hours to days, so blood flow can come back. One is an antibiotic powder (imipenem and cilastatin) that forms very fine particles. In some countries, doctors also use dissolving beads made of gelatin. A seaweed-based gel (alginate) is being tested in early studies.48– to 51

    They break down. Early studies suggest knee pain improves about the same with the antibiotic powder as with beads that stay.49, 52

    Used for knee arthritis

Some beads contain gelatin from pigs. If you avoid pork products or have had a reaction to gelatin, tell your doctor. Types without gelatin are available.39– to 41

Bottom line

Early results are encouraging. Most people bleed less, and about 2 out of 3 people still have little or no bleeding a year later. Some need a second treatment or surgery. It is mainly a treatment for bleeding.3, 4, 19– to 22

What the studies found

  • Most bleed less at first. Pooled data from 13 studies of 381 patients: it worked for about 8 out of 10 (82%; range 73% to 89%). Every study followed one group before and after, with no comparison group.21
  • Results vary from center to center. A review of 22 studies (810 procedures) found success from 63% to 94%, and repeat treatment in 8% to 20%. The authors judged the studies too different to combine into one number.4
  • The benefit fades for some within a year.
    • 80 patients in a registry: 68.7% had no bleeding at 12 months; 21.3% had a second embolization and 5% had surgery.19
    • 25 patients: 72% success at 12 months, but 8 of the 18 successes needed 2 sessions.20
    • 32 patients, with a strict definition of success: 87.5% at 1 month, 59.4% at 6 months, 56.2% at 12 months.22
  • Against surgery: far less pain early. One small trial of 29 people compared embolization with closed hemorrhoid removal. Pain at the first bowel movement was 0 after embolization vs 6.08 after surgery (0 means no pain), and pain-medicine doses 2.4 vs 28.9. At 1 year, symptoms remained in 6 of 13 vs 5 of 13, and 8 of 13 vs 13 of 13 were satisfied; the trial was too small to compare results at 1 year. Its limits: one center, patients and doctors knew the treatment, coils only, designed to measure pain alone, and a device maker supplied grants and equipment.3
  • In everyday U.S. practice: 134 patients at one outpatient center, about half after earlier banding. All went home 2 hours after; success was 93% at 1 month, with no longer follow-up. The first author consults for device makers.23
  • In frail patients: 21 patients at high surgical risk with anemia (a low blood count): hemoglobin rose 1.2 g/dL on average. 3 still needed blood transfusions.24
  • Bulging: grade did not change in the particle-size trial, and other series report small or short-lived changes. The procedure does not lift tissue (our inference from how it works).4, 5

Harms

  • Common and short-lived: a few days of rectal pressure, urge to go, or low fever. In one series, 53.1% had an urge to go and 34.4% a low fever; in another, 81.8% had an urge to go that eased in 3 to 5 days.25, 26
  • Small ulcers, which healed: in a small trial that looked inside everyone with a scope, shallow ulcers were found in 71.4% of patients with the smallest particles and 0% with the largest, all healed by 6 months. 3 of 14 in the smallest-particle group had ulcers higher up in the bowel (the rectosigmoid) that needed enema treatment and healed after 6 months. Most series do not look with a scope, so they undercount ulcers.5
  • Serious bowel injury: possible, but rare. It has been reported more than once, with no reliable rate. Case reports describe bowel damage from poor blood flow, including one hole in the bowel that needed an operation and one narrowing of the bowel.4, 27– to 29
  • Loss of bowel control has not been reported.4

Who was studied, and who it is for

Studied mostly in people with bleeding hemorrhoids of grades 1 to 3 that did not respond to other care, some too sick for surgery, some who refused it. Pregnancy, active inflammatory bowel disease, poor kidney function, and dye allergy are commonly excluded from trials. It is not a treatment for a clotted hemorrhoid or a fissure.4, 7, 30

What experts say

  • NICE (England), last reviewed 2018 and based on 84 patients, says it should be used only in research; its next review is due April 2027.31
  • An Italian colorectal consensus reserves it for selected patients with disabling symptoms that other care has not helped, and no bulging that cannot be pushed back.32
  • The AGA review's summary does not mention it. We found no position from the U.S. or European interventional radiology societies (SIR or CIRSE).7

What is not known

  • How it compares with banding or artery tying.4
  • Results past about 2 years, where almost no data exist.4, 19
  • Trials under way: against banding or artery tying (80 patients, results expected 2030), against banding (40 patients, 2030), and against hemorrhoid removal (102 patients, 2028).30, 33, 34
  • If it works for you, it may spare you surgery. No study has measured that against a comparison group.

Banding

How well it works
Good evidence suggests Works well for many; comes back more often than after surgery
What it involves
A rubber band placed in the office
Recovery
Many back at work the next day
Hemorrhoids that bulge out
Helps; may last longer than infrared

A rubber band is placed above the pain-sensing line inside the anus, around the base of an internal hemorrhoid, which then shrinks and falls off.6, 7

Bottom line

Good evidence suggests banding works well for many people. Hemorrhoids come back more often than after surgery, and some people need more than one session.1, 53

What the studies found

  • Against hemorrhoid removal: one trial across several hospitals, in 87 people with grade 3 hemorrhoids, stopped early:1
    • Hemorrhoids came back in about half after banding (48.8%) vs about 1 out of 16 after removal (6.1%), with up to 2 banding sessions allowed.1
    • Back to work after 1 day with banding vs 9 days after removal.1
    • Pain in the first week: 1 after banding vs 4 after removal, on a pain score where 0 means no pain.1
  • Against infrared: see infrared treatment below.54

Harms

Pain and pressure for a few days, and some bleeding when the band falls off. A serious pelvic infection is rare after any hemorrhoid treatment.7, 54

What experts say

The AGA advises banding or infrared treatment before surgery for grades 1 to 3, and a surgeon's opinion for grade 3 hemorrhoids that do not respond to banding.7

What is not known

How banding compares with embolization; trials are under way.30, 33

Infrared treatment

How well it works
Studies disagree About as good as banding at first
What it involves
Heat from a probe, in the office
Recovery
Less pain than banding
Hemorrhoids that bulge out
Banding may last longer

Infrared light from a probe scars the hemorrhoid so it shrinks.6

Bottom line

Studies disagree on whether it lasts as long as banding. It helps about as much as banding at first.7, 53, 54

What the studies found

  • About the same as banding: 9 randomized trials of moderate quality compared banding with heat or coagulation methods, including infrared. They found no clear difference in success, return of hemorrhoids, or bulging. For infrared alone, banding caused more pain, and bleeding afterward was no different.54
  • Fewer repeat treatments after banding: an older pooled analysis of 5 trials (863 patients) found similar numbers symptom-free at 12 months, but fewer people needed repeat treatment after banding.53

Harms

Less pain than banding; bleeding afterward was no different.54

What experts say

The AGA says banding gives longer benefit for bulging and repeat bleeding, and, for people with cirrhosis whose blood clotting is badly impaired, prefers infrared over banding.7

Injection (sclerotherapy)

How well it works
Early studies suggest About as good as banding and other non-surgical treatments
What it involves
An injection, in the office

Not stated in our sources
Hemorrhoids that bulge out
Not stated in our sources

A chemical injected into the hemorrhoid scars it.55

Bottom line

Early studies suggest it works about as well as banding and other non-surgical treatments.55

What the studies found

14 randomized trials compared injection with other non-surgical treatments: banding, heat or electric treatments, fiber, flavonoid pills, other injection mixes, and artery tying. The authors pooled these comparisons together, and results varied a lot from trial to trial:55

  • Same success: success was equally likely with injection and with the other treatments (risk ratio 1.00; range 0.71 to 1.41).55
  • Fewer complications: complications were about half as likely with injection (risk ratio 0.46).55

What is not known

Long-term results; the authors call for more research.55

Hemorrhoid removal vs. stapled, laser, and artery-tying surgery

Surgery ties off, lifts, shrinks, or removes the hemorrhoids. It is the most lasting fix, and recovery hurts more.

Artery tying (ligation)

How well it works
Early studies suggest Helps; comes back more often than after removal
What it involves
Stitches tie off the arteries, inside the anus
Recovery
Faster than after removal; more pain than banding
Hemorrhoids that bulge out
Not stated in our sources

Doppler-guided artery ligation, also called dearterialization: an ultrasound probe in the anus finds the feeding arteries, which are tied with stitches; a lifting stitch (mucopexy) is sometimes added.56, 57

Bottom line

Early studies suggest it helps, but hemorrhoids come back more often than after removal. It may last longer than a single banding.56, 57

What the studies found

  • Against banding: a UK trial of 372 people in 17 centers:57
    • Hemorrhoids back at 1 year: 30% after artery tying vs 49% after a single banding.57
    • Most of that gap came from people who needed repeat banding. Repeat banding made the comparison 30% vs 37.5%, which could be due to chance.57
    • Serious problems needing a hospital stay: 12 people (7%) after artery tying vs 2 (1%) after banding.57
    • More pain on day 1 and day 7 after artery tying.57
  • Against removal: in 6 randomized trials of 465 patients, hemorrhoids came back more often after artery tying than after removal; the odds of return were almost 3 times as high (odds ratio 2.76; range 1.03 to 7.38). People got back to normal about 14 days sooner after artery tying.56

Harms

More pain than banding in the first week; serious problems needing a hospital stay were more common after artery tying than after banding in the UK trial.57

What is not known

It targets the same blood supply as embolization, so how long it lasts may behave alike (our inference; no trial tests this).56

Stapled surgery

How well it works
Good evidence suggests Bulging comes back more often than after removal
What it involves
A stapling tool lifts the hemorrhoids
Recovery
Not stated in our sources
Hemorrhoids that bulge out
Comes back more often than after removal

A circular stapler removes a ring of tissue above the hemorrhoids and lifts them back into place.58

Bottom line

Good evidence suggests symptoms, mostly bulging, come back more often than after standard surgery.58

What the studies found

17 randomized trials of 2,072 patients compared stapled surgery with hemorrhoid removal:58

  • Bulging came back about 3 times as often after stapled surgery (risk ratio 3.28; range 1.49 to 7.25).58
  • Symptoms overall came back about 1.5 times as often (risk ratio 1.56; range 1.00 to 2.44). The low end of the range is no difference, so this part could be due to chance.58
  • The authors judge that there are still too few patients to be sure.

Harms

Long-term pain, bowel function, and quality of life were about the same as after hemorrhoid removal.58

Laser heat shrinks the hemorrhoids

Back to daily life sooner than after removal
Hemorrhoids that bulge out
Not yet known

A laser fiber shrinks the hemorrhoid from inside.59

Bottom line

Early studies suggest it hurts less than standard surgery. How long it lasts is not yet known.59

What the studies found

15 randomized trials of 1,099 patients compared laser surgery with hemorrhoid removal:59

  • Less pain: lower pain on day 1 and day 7 after laser surgery.59
  • Faster recovery: back to daily life about 11 days sooner.59
  • How sure: low to very low certainty, with short follow-up.

Less bleeding, trouble passing urine, and narrowing of the anus than after removal in the pooled trials.59

Long-term results; the authors do not suggest it replace removal.59

Hemorrhoid removal (hemorrhoidectomy)

How well it works
Good evidence suggests The most lasting fix
What it involves
Surgery to cut out the hemorrhoids
Recovery
Painful first week or more
Hemorrhoids that bulge out
Treats them; needed when they stay out

The hemorrhoids are cut out, with the wounds closed or left open.2

Bottom line

Good evidence suggests it is the most lasting fix. Hemorrhoids come back less often than after banding or other procedures.1, 2

What the studies found

  • Fewest hemorrhoid returns, most pain: a combined analysis of 79 randomized trials (9,232 patients) found closed and open removal, along with stapled surgery, had the fewest returns of hemorrhoids and the most pain of the procedures compared. 73% of the trials had flaws that could skew their results.2
  • Against banding: hemorrhoids came back in 6.1% after removal vs 48.8% after banding, in one trial of 87 people; see banding above.1

Harms

Pain, bleeding, and trouble passing urine are more common than after office procedures. Lasting loss of some bowel control: about 2 to 3 out of 100 people in randomized trials (2.48%; range 1.09% to 4.25%).2, 60

What experts say

The AGA says grade 4 hemorrhoids need surgical removal.7

Does insurance cover hemorrhoid embolization?

Coverage varies by plan.61– to 64

  • Cigna's guideline covers it under set conditions after failed non-surgical care.
  • UnitedHealthcare lists it as unproven in a policy effective December 1, 2026.62
  • Aetna lists it as experimental.
  • Medicare decides case by case.
  • Many insurers have no written policy.
