How to read this page

This page is for readers who want the studies behind the short guide. Each treatment starts with the conclusion and how sure we are, in the same certainty words the short guide uses. Then come the study sizes, designs, comparison groups, follow-up, harms and how they were counted, who was studied, funding, and what is not known.

Numbers from different studies are not head-to-head comparisons. They come from different people, follow-up times, and outcome measures, so this page never lines them up as if they did.

When a study gives a range after a number, the true result most likely falls inside that range. A wide range means the study could not pin the result down.

Every Option's physician performs knee embolization. It is held to the same standard of evidence as every other option here.

What is knee arthritis?

In arthritis, the cushion between the bones wears away. The bones can rub together. This causes pain, swelling, and stiffness. Arthritis is the most common disease of the knee.1, 2

Common symptoms1

  • Pain when you walk or put weight on the knee
  • Swelling
  • Stiffness after waking, often 30 minutes or less
  • Later, pain at rest or at night
When to see a doctor

See a doctor if your symptoms bother you or get worse. A doctor can check what is causing your symptoms and go over your options.1

Exercise vs. medicines

These are the first steps for most people. No procedure is needed.

Exercise and weight loss

How well it works
Good evidence suggests Better use of the knee; pain 8.7 points better than placebo on a 100-point scale, under the 12-point smallest change that matters
What it involves
An exercise program, often with a physical therapist
Recovery
None
Keeps your own knee?
Yes

Strengthening and range-of-motion exercise, usually guided by a physical therapist, plus weight loss for people who are overweight. It is the usual first step.1

Bottom line

Good evidence suggests exercise improves knee function. Its effect on pain is small. On its own, it is not clear the pain effect is big enough to matter. The effect is larger when weight loss is added.3, 4

The studies

  • Exercise helps knee function; its effect on pain is small. A Cochrane review (a careful summary of the trials) found pain 8.7 points better on a 100-point scale than with placebo or attention control (a fake treatment, or time with staff but no exercise), with low certainty. Pain was 13.1 points better than with no treatment (low certainty), and 10.4 points better when exercise was added to another treatment (moderate certainty). The smallest change patients notice (the minimal important difference) is 12 points. Function improved, with moderate certainty.3
  • Diet plus exercise eased pain more than either one alone. A publicly funded trial of 454 overweight or obese adults aged 55 and over, followed 18 months, compared diet plus exercise, exercise alone, and diet alone. Diet plus exercise led to 10.6 kg (11.4%) weight loss. Pain was lower with diet plus exercise (3.6 on a 0 to 20 scale, where higher means more pain) than with exercise alone (4.7) or diet alone (4.8).4

Harms, and how they were counted

Pain can make it hard to keep active. Ask for a routine you can keep up.1

Who was studied, and who it is for

A patient source advises reassessing the plan if physical therapy has not helped after several weeks. That is a reason to review the program with a therapist, not to stop being active.1

Pain relievers

How well it works
Strong evidence shows Anti-inflammatory pills ease knee pain; acetaminophen adds very little
What it involves
Pills or skin gels
Recovery
None
Keeps your own knee?
Yes

Acetaminophen, anti-inflammatory pills (NSAIDs) such as ibuprofen and naproxen, and NSAID gels on the skin.1

Bottom line

Strong evidence shows anti-inflammatory pills ease knee pain. Gels help some people, with fewer side effects. Acetaminophen adds very little. None of them changes the joint itself.1, 5, 6

The studies

  • Anti-inflammatory pills and opioids were compared in one large review. It pooled randomized trials (people assigned to a treatment by chance) of anti-inflammatory pills (NSAIDs) and opioids in knee and hip arthritis.5
  • Acetaminophen helps too little to matter for most people. A review pooled trials against a placebo (a pill with no active ingredient). It found acetaminophen's effect on arthritis pain too small to matter for most people.6

Harms, and how they were counted

Do not take more than 4,000 mg of acetaminophen a day. Anti-inflammatory pills raise the risk of heart attack, stroke, and stomach bleeding. Talk with a doctor before taking them regularly.1, 7

Other medicines

How well it works
Good evidence suggests Duloxetine eases long-term pain a little; opioids only slightly
What it involves
Daily prescription pills
Recovery
None
Keeps your own knee?
Yes

Duloxetine, a prescription pill also used for depression and nerve pain; and opioid pills, including tramadol.1

Bottom line

Good evidence suggests duloxetine eases long-term knee pain a little. Opioids ease arthritis pain only slightly.8– to 10

The studies

  • Duloxetine helps a little, but more people stop it for side effects. A review pooled randomized trials of duloxetine in osteoarthritis. It found a small benefit. More people stopped for side effects than with placebo.8
  • Opioids and tramadol help only a little. Cochrane reviews of opioids and of tramadol found small benefits. Most tramadol trials were funded by industry.9, 10

Harms, and how they were counted

Side effects make some people stop duloxetine. For most people with arthritis, opioid harms outweigh the small benefit.5, 8, 9

GAE vs. shots and nerve ablation

These are done without surgery. You keep your own knee. Before you agree to surgery, ask about these.

Knee artery embolization (GAE)

Does insurance cover it? →

How well it works
Early results are encouraging Pain fell about 37 points on a 100-point scale at 12 months, in studies mostly without a comparison group; against a sham, not clearly better when the blinded trials are pooled
What it involves
A thin tube through a tiny skin nick to the knee arteries
Recovery
Home the same day
Keeps your own knee?
Yes

Genicular artery embolization (GAE) blocks small abnormal vessels that grow into the inflamed lining of the knee (synovium). An interventional radiologist passes a thin tube (catheter), usually from the groin, with numbing medicine (local anesthetic) or light sedation. The doctor injects small particles into the genicular arteries feeding those vessels. You go home the same day. The idea is that this calms the inflamed lining and leaves fewer nerve endings beside the new vessels. That is a proposed explanation, not a proven one.17, 46– to 48

What is used to block the blood flow?

Embolization means blocking blood flow on purpose, from inside a blood vessel. A doctor guides a thin tube to the spot and releases a material that blocks the small blood vessels feeding it. Doctors choose the material by the size of the vessels and how long the blockage should last.30

  • Tiny beads (microspheres)

    Round beads of plastic. One common type is an acrylic plastic with gelatin from pigs. Others are a water-holding plastic gel (polyvinyl alcohol) or a plastic gel with a thin coating.31– to 33

    They do not dissolve. They stay in place for good. They are not made of metal and do not stop you from having an MRI.31, 32, 34

    Used for knee arthritisAlso used for fibroids, enlarged prostate, and hemorrhoids

  • Materials that dissolve

    An antibiotic powder (imipenem and cilastatin) that forms very fine particles. It breaks down within hours to days, so blood flow can come back.35, 36

    They break down. Early studies suggest knee pain improves about the same with the antibiotic powder as with beads that stay.36, 37

    Used for knee arthritis

Other materials doctors use
  • Small particles (polyvinyl alcohol)

    Rough, uneven flakes of a plastic.38

    They stay in place for good.38

    Used for fibroids and enlarged prostate

  • Tiny metal coils

    Small springs of platinum or another metal, often with soft fibers. During prostate embolization, doctors sometimes place one in a nearby artery so the beads do not reach the tissue it feeds.30, 39– to 41

    They stay in place for good. They are safe in an MRI scanner under set conditions; tell the scanner staff you have them.39

    Used for enlarged prostate, hemorrhoids, and chronic pelvic pain

  • Plugs

    A small, self-opening plug of fine wire mesh, made of nitinol (a nickel and titanium metal).42

    It stays in place for good. It is safe in an MRI scanner under set conditions; tell the scanner staff you have it.42

    Used for chronic pelvic pain

  • Medical glue (n-butyl cyanoacrylate)

    A liquid glue that the doctor injects into the vein.43

    It hardens quickly and blocks the vein.30

    Used for chronic pelvic pain

  • Vein-closing medicine (sclerosant)

    A medicine that works like a detergent, often mixed into a foam (sodium tetradecyl sulfate or polidocanol). It irritates the inside lining of the vein.44, 45

    The vein scars shut. This is not always permanent.44

    Used for chronic pelvic pain

Some beads contain gelatin from pigs. If you avoid pork products or have had a reaction to gelatin, tell your doctor. Types without gelatin are available.31– to 33

Bottom line

Early results are encouraging. Studies without a comparison group consistently report large drops in pain that last at least a year. However, in the blinded trials (where patients did not know which they had), people who had a sham (fake) procedure also improved a lot. When those trials are pooled, GAE was not clearly better than sham. Larger trials are under way.11– to 13

The studies

Trials against a sham (fake) procedure, where patients did not know which one they had:

  • Pooled, the sham trials did not show a clear benefit. Three sham-controlled trials have reported, with 138 people in all, measured at different times (1, 4 and 12 months). Pooled together, people who had GAE had slightly less pain than people who had the sham, but the difference could be due to chance (standardized mean difference -0.23; range -0.71 to 0.25). The range allows anything from a moderate true benefit to none.11
  • The first and smallest sham trial favored GAE in the first month; after that, no sham group was left to compare. 21 people with mild to moderate arthritis at several centres were assigned by chance, 2 to GAE for every 1 to sham. Patients did not know which they had (single-blind). At 1 month, pain on a 100 mm scale fell 50.1 mm more with GAE than with sham (range 29.0 to 72.3). Every sham patient then switched to GAE, so there is no blinded comparison after 1 month. 5 people were left out for taking more pain medicine. Its grant list names a device maker.14
  • In early arthritis, GAE and sham both helped, and the difference could be due to chance. 59 people with early arthritis (Kellgren-Lawrence grade 2 on X-ray) at one centre, followed 12 months. Patients and the people measuring results did not know who had GAE (triple-blind). Knee pain (KOOS score) improved 41.3% with GAE and 29.4% with sham. On the main and the other outcomes, the difference could be due to chance. In a group whose arteries were fully blocked (17 people, against 29 controls), 76.5% against 37.9% felt moderately or much better. That group was not chosen by chance, so it is a lead, not a result. Funding is not stated in the record.15
  • In the largest completed sham trial, both groups improved, with no clear difference. 58 people (29 GAE, 29 sham) with Kellgren-Lawrence grade 1 to 3 arthritis, at one centre, with one doctor doing every procedure. Neither the patients nor the people measuring results knew who had GAE (double-blind), and no one dropped out. At 4 months, knee pain (KOOS score, 0 to 100) improved 21.4 points with GAE and 18.4 with sham. The difference of 3.0 points could be due to chance (range -7.1 to 13.0). Both groups improved by more than 12 points, the smallest change patients notice (the minimal important difference). 3 of 29 people in the GAE group had no vessels to treat and were not embolized. They were still counted in the GAE group, as the trial design requires. Grant support includes a device maker.16
    • Still no clear difference a year later. At 12 months, the difference was 7.46 points, which could be due to chance (range -13.63 to 28.56). Inflammation of the joint lining on MRI (synovitis), checked up to 4 months, did not change. The authors read this as a lasting placebo effect. However, the top of the range still allows a benefit larger than the 12-point smallest change patients notice, and published letters dispute the authors' reading.17
  • Against usual care alone, with no sham, GAE did better. 50 people (Kellgren-Lawrence grade 2 to 4) were assigned by chance to GAE plus usual care or to usual care alone. Everyone knew which they had (not blinded). The procedure could be completed in 82.8% of knees (24 of 29). At 1 year, pain on a visual scale improved 72.7% with GAE against 24.2% with usual care alone. A pain flare lasting about 2 weeks followed in 7 of 28 embolized knees, and one shallow abscess (a pocket of infection) needed treatment. With no sham group, this cannot separate the procedure from placebo.18

Studies without a comparison group cannot separate the treatment from placebo. Read them beside the sham results above.

  • Pooled, pain fell a lot. A review pooled 45 studies and 2,205 patients, mostly without a comparison group. Pain fell about 37 points on a 100-point scale at 12 months. The reviewers rated their certainty in this as very low to low.12
  • One U.S. centre: the response often lasted, but some people lost it. 40 people with moderate to severe arthritis at one U.S. centre. At 2 years, 18 of 38 (47.4%) had at least a 50% drop in their WOMAC score (pain, stiffness and daily function). Of the 25 who had responded at 1 year, 18 (72.0%) still responded at 2 years and 7 (28.0%) had lost the benefit. Grant support includes a device maker.19
  • One UK study: pain fell, but many people had no long-term results. 46 people treated with permanent beads in the UK. Average pain on a 100-point scale fell from 58.6 to 37.7 at 2 years. However, only 28 of 46 had 2-year results. On MRI, the overall joint damage score got worse, while inflammation of the joint lining (synovitis) fell. Funded by a device maker.20
  • One Japanese study: high success, without saying what success meant. 72 people (95 knees) in Japan with Kellgren-Lawrence grade 1 to 3 arthritis, mostly treated with a temporary blocking agent. Over time, the study reported success in 86.3% at 6 months and 79.8% at 3 years. The abstract does not say what counted as success.21

Harms, and how they were counted

Temporary skin color change or redness over the knee affects about 1 to 3 out of 10 people (10% to 30% in the society's summary of more than 1,000 reported cases). It usually fades within a month. The society's summary puts it at about 0.3% get a small skin sore (ulcer), and fewer than 0.1% a small patch of bone that lost its blood supply (bone infarct) seen on MRI, usually without symptoms. Most reports find no permanent nerve injury, joint infection or bone death.12, 22, 23

In a UCLA group treated with 100-micron permanent beads, 7 of 40 had a small skin sore that healed on its own. 2 of 40 had a bone infarct that caused no symptoms. A 2026 review that pooled studies found small patches of bone death without symptoms only with permanent beads (microspheres). Across the studies, 18.3% had minor side effects. In the 58-person sham trial, half of whom had GAE, there were 23 mild side effects in the GAE group and 5 in the sham group. 11 of 29 people who had GAE had brief skin redness (gone in 1 to 4 weeks). 2 of 29 had numbness or tingling at the outer ankle. In one person it had gone by 8 months; in the other it was still there at 12 months. A German national registry recorded 11 complications in 2,335 procedures (0.5%) at 109 centres. Its authors call this a minimum, because the registry was not built to catch complications.11, 16, 17, 24, 25

Embolization studies look for and report skin changes that surgical series rarely list, and knee replacement harms are defined differently, so these rates are not comparable with surgical complication rates.

Who was studied, and who it is for

Trials usually required age over 40, pain above 4 out of 10, and at least 3 months of non-surgical care that had not helped. They left out people with significant artery disease in the legs, pain coming from the spine, or fibromyalgia (long-term widespread pain). In severe (grade 4) arthritis, some studies found it worked less well and others found equal results.22

The sham trials enrolled people with mild to moderate arthritis. That is where studies without a comparison group look best, and where placebo responses are large. People having GAE in the U.S. are often older, have more severe arthritis, or cannot have or do not want surgery. So the trials may not represent them. That is our inference: the papers do not report it.15, 16, 22

On who does best, studies without a comparison group conflict. In one 33-person study, 81.8% of people with mild X-ray changes responded at 6 months, against 36.4% of people with moderate to severe changes. Yet the sham trials that found no clear benefit were in exactly those milder knees. So the difference may be largely placebo.16, 26

What experts say

The Society of Interventional Radiology says GAE should be offered to people with knee arthritis pain when non-surgical treatment has not helped, and who cannot have, do not want, or want to delay a knee replacement. Most of its authors report paid consulting for device makers. The statement does not cite the larger sham trial that found no clear benefit. It calls for larger randomized trials.22

What is not known

  • Whether GAE beats sham in large enough trials. Trials under way include sham-controlled trials of 110 people (already enrolled), 130 people and 40 people; a three-arm trial of 150 people comparing GAE with nerve treatment and with sham; trials against a steroid shot; and a 117-person trial against watching and waiting (observation).22
  • Whether it delays knee replacement. No study has measured time to replacement against a group chosen by chance. In U.S. insurance claims, 13.1% of people had a knee replacement within 12 months after GAE, against 12.8% of similar (matched) people who did not have GAE: no difference. A 12-month claims comparison cannot show whether surgery is delayed.27
  • How often people need a repeat treatment. In a pooled analysis of 9 studies (270 patients), about 8% had a repeat GAE within up to 2 years.28
  • Which blocking material works best. The one trial that assigned blocking materials by chance, in 60 people with mild to moderate arthritis followed 12 months, found no difference between a temporary material and permanent beads (microspheres). Pain at 1 year was 3.95 with the temporary material and 3.88 with the beads, out of 10.29
  • Whether it slows arthritis. In one study without a comparison group, the MRI damage score got worse.20

Steroid shot

How well it works
Studies disagree Relief for a while, but the relief usually fades by about 3 months
What it involves
A shot during an office visit
Recovery
None
Keeps your own knee?
Yes

A corticosteroid injected into the knee during an office visit.1

Bottom line

Studies disagree on how much a steroid shot helps. Relief is real in the short term, but the relief usually fades by about 3 months. Repeat shots do not add relief. In one trial, physical therapy did better over a year.1, 49– to 51

The studies

  • Relief is moderate at first and fades within months. A Cochrane review of 27 trials (1,767 people) found a moderate benefit 1 to 2 weeks after the shot (standardized mean difference -0.48). The benefit shrank to -0.22 at 13 weeks and to almost nothing (-0.07) at 26 weeks. Most trials were small and of low quality.49
  • Repeat shots thinned cartilage without easing pain more. In a publicly funded trial of 140 people at one centre, people had either a steroid shot or a salt-water (saline) shot every 3 months for 2 years. Cartilage thinned more with steroid than with saline (-0.21 mm against -0.10 mm). Pain did not ease more with steroid (-1.2 against -1.9). The saline group also improved.50
  • Physical therapy did better than steroid shots over a year. In a trial of 156 people, people who had physical therapy did better at 1 year than people who had steroid shots, on a score of pain, stiffness and daily function (WOMAC difference 18.8; range 5.0 to 32.6).51

Harms, and how they were counted

Repeat shots may thin the cartilage a little faster (in the trial above). Before a knee replacement: Across 8 studies that looked back at past records, people who had a shot within 3 months before knee replacement were somewhat more likely to get a joint infection than people who did not (risk ratio 1.26; range 1.18 to 1.35). No link was found for shots given more than 3 months before.50, 52

Gel shots (hyaluronic acid)

How well it works
Strong evidence shows Pain about 2 points better than placebo on a 100-point scale, too little to notice
What it involves
Shots during office visits
Recovery
None
Keeps your own knee?
Yes

A hyaluronic acid gel injected into the knee, meant to supplement the joint fluid that thins in arthritis.48

Bottom line

Strong evidence shows gel shots ease pain only slightly more than placebo shots (shots with no active ingredient). The difference is too small for most people to notice.53

The studies

  • Gel shots barely beat placebo, and serious side effects were more common. A review of 169 trials (21,163 people) looked at the 24 large trials (8,997 people) separately. In those, pain was about 2 mm lower than with placebo on a 100 mm scale (standardized mean difference -0.08), less than the smallest change patients notice. People having gel shots were about one and a half times as likely to have a serious side effect as people having placebo shots (risk ratio 1.49; range 1.12 to 1.98).53

Harms, and how they were counted

Brief soreness or swelling of the knee. Serious side effects were more common than with placebo shots in the same review.48, 53

Platelet-rich plasma (PRP)

How well it works
Studies disagree It may ease pain for up to 6 months; no better than saline at 12 months in the largest placebo-controlled trial
What it involves
A blood draw, then shots
Recovery
None
Keeps your own knee?
Yes

Platelet-rich plasma (PRP) is prepared from your own blood and injected into the knee.48, 55

Bottom line

Studies disagree on whether PRP helps. A review of smaller trials suggests it may ease pain for up to 6 months, but with low certainty. However, the largest placebo-controlled trial found no difference from salt water (saline) at one year.54, 55

The studies

  • In the largest trial, PRP did no better than salt water. The largest trial against a placebo, 288 people aged 50 and over with Kellgren-Lawrence grade 2 to 3 arthritis, compared PRP with salt-water (saline) shots. At 12 months, pain fell 2.1 points with PRP and 1.8 with saline (difference -0.4; range -0.9 to 0.2). The difference could be due to chance, and it was well below the 1.8-point smallest change patients notice. The trial tested one product with few white blood cells (leukocyte-poor).55

Harms, and how they were counted

Side effects were mild and short-lived in the largest trial.55

Nerve ablation

How well it works
Studies disagree It may ease pain for about 3 months; no different from a sham at 12 months in one double-blind trial
What it involves
Heat applied to the knee's pain nerves
Recovery
Not well reported
Keeps your own knee?
Yes

Radiofrequency heat applied to the genicular nerves that carry pain from the knee. Our sources do not describe the procedure steps or recovery well enough to state them.48

Bottom line

Studies disagree on whether it helps. Pooled trials suggest it may ease pain for about 3 months. However, longer relief has not been shown, and the one long trial against a sham found no benefit.56, 57

The studies

  • The trials are weak overall. A review of 25 trials rated its certainty in the results low to very low.56
  • Against a sham, no difference at one year. A trial of 64 people compared it with a sham, and neither the patients nor the people measuring results knew which they had (double-blind). It found no difference at 12 months.57
  • Against a steroid shot, not a sham, more people had at least half their pain gone. A trial of 151 people compared it with a single steroid shot, not a sham. At 6 months, 74.1% of people who had nerve ablation had at least half their pain gone, against 16.2% of people who had the steroid shot.58

Harms, and how they were counted

Serious problems seem no more common than with a sham procedure.56

Why the comparison group matters

Knee pain improves a great deal with placebo, and more with procedures than with pills. This is not special to embolization. Trials that compared a treatment with a placebo show it:

  • Steroid shots: salt-water (saline) shots did nearly as well as repeated steroid shots over two years.50
  • PRP: saline did nearly as well as PRP at one year.55
  • Gel shots: they did little better than placebo.53
  • Knee scope: sham surgery matched washout through a scope (arthroscopic washout).59

So a large improvement in a study without a comparison group tells you people felt better, not how much of that came from the treatment itself.11

Embolization for other joints

None of these studies had a sham group, and no knee number applies to another joint.60– to 62

  • Hip: a study without a comparison group followed people for a year. Another study compared groups that were not chosen by chance, in people who could not have surgery.60, 61
  • Hand and fingers: one study of 9 people with no comparison group.62
  • Shoulder: a formal review of the evidence (health technology assessment) found no studies of embolization for shoulder arthritis.63
  • Frozen shoulder: it is not arthritis. It often improves on its own, though this can take up to 3 years. Studies without a comparison group, and a review that pooled studies, report less night pain within weeks. Serious problems are possible, but rare: brief loss of vision has been reported.64– to 66

Knee replacement vs. other surgery

Surgery replaces the worn parts of the knee or changes how weight rests on it.

Knee replacement

How well it works
Strong evidence shows Most new knees last a long time; eased pain more than a non-surgical program at 1 year in the one randomized trial
What it involves
Surgery and a hospital stay
Recovery
Home in 1 to 4 days; daily activities in 3 to 6 weeks
Keeps your own knee?
No. The worn surfaces are replaced

Surgery replaces the worn cartilage and bone with metal and plastic parts. Surgery takes 1 to 2 hours. Most people leave hospital in 1 to 4 days. Most daily activities return in 3 to 6 weeks. Full recovery can take months.72, 75

Bottom line

Strong evidence shows most new knees last a long time: about 8 out of 10 still have their original parts 25 years later (implant survival, not pain relief). For moderate to severe arthritis, one randomized trial found it eases pain more than a non-surgical program. However, about 1 to 2 out of 10 people are not satisfied (19% of 1,703 knees in an older Ontario study that counted neutral answers; 10% averaged across 21 studies from 2010 to 2022).67– to 70

The studies

  • Surgery eased pain more than a non-surgical program, but serious side effects were more common. The one trial that assigned people by chance to replacement or to a non-surgical program included 100 people with moderate to severe arthritis. At 1 year, replacement eased pain more. However, serious side effects were more common with surgery.70
  • Many who tried the non-surgical program first had still not had surgery. In a parallel trial of people who could have had a replacement, many who did the non-surgical program still had not had surgery 2 years later.71

Harms, and how they were counted

Serious problems such as a joint infection happen in fewer than 2 out of 100 people. Some people have lasting pain or stiffness.72, 73

Who was studied, and who it is for

Timing: for people who have chosen surgery after non-surgical care did not help, a 2023 guideline from the American College of Rheumatology and the hip and knee surgeons' society advises against delaying surgery to try more non-surgical treatment. It is a conditional (weaker) recommendation, based on low or very low quality evidence.74

Partial knee replacement

How well it works
Results about the same as a full replacement at 5 years, in one randomized trial of 528 people
What it involves
Surgery on one part of the knee
Recovery
Shorter than a full replacement
Keeps your own knee?
Partly

Surgery replaces only the worn compartment of the knee, usually the inner side.1, 76

Bottom line

It is an option when only one part of the knee is worn. In the main trial, results matched a full replacement in the medium term. However, a partial knee is more likely to need redoing later.67, 76

The studies

  • Partial and total replacement did about as well. A trial assigned 528 people with arthritis on the inner side of the knee by chance to partial or total replacement. Results at 5 years were about the same.76

Harms, and how they were counted

The same kinds of risks as a full replacement: infection, blood clots, and ongoing pain or stiffness. Recovery is shorter: driving after about 3 weeks instead of 6.73, 75

Bone realignment (osteotomy)

How well it works
Early studies suggest Eases symptoms more than non-surgical care, in one trial
What it involves
Surgery to reshape the shin bone
Recovery
Not well reported
Keeps your own knee?
Yes

High tibial osteotomy reshapes the shin bone to shift weight off a worn inner compartment. It is mainly for younger, active people with bow legs.77, 78

Bottom line

Early studies suggest it eases symptoms more than non-surgical care. This comes from one randomized trial at one centre, which also followed a group of people who chose their own treatment.77

The studies

  • Some people later have a knee replacement. In a review that pooled follow-up studies, about 1 out of 10 people have a knee replacement more than 10 years later.78

Harms, and how they were counted

Our sources do not give its complication rate or recovery time; ask the surgeon.78

Knee scope clean-out

How well it works
Strong evidence shows Does not help knee arthritis; a major guideline advises against it
What it involves
Camera surgery through small cuts
Recovery
Not well reported
Keeps your own knee?
Yes

Arthroscopic washout or trimming (debridement) through small cuts with a camera.1, 59

Bottom line

Strong evidence shows a knee scope clean-out does not help knee arthritis. A major orthopedic guideline advises against it.59, 79, 80

The studies

  • No better than a sham operation. In a trial with a sham (fake) operation, washout and trimming (debridement) did no better than the sham. All groups improved.59
  • No added benefit over physical therapy and medicines. A second randomized trial found the scope added nothing to physical and medical therapy.79

Harms, and how they were counted

It carries the risks of surgery, including blood clots and infection.81

Does a less invasive step affect later surgery?

With knee arthritis, waiting does not put your life or leg at risk. Guidelines step from exercise and medicines to injections, and then surgery. However, knee function can decline while waiting.1, 74

  • After GAE: In 48 knee replacements done on average 512 days after GAE, 8.3% had a side effect or complication and 4.2% a wound-healing problem. All of these resolved. There was no comparison group, and the authors perform GAE themselves. How well people do after the later surgery has not been studied.82
  • After a steroid shot: a shot in the three months before surgery was linked to more infection (see the steroid section).52
